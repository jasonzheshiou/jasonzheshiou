I make AI decisions in insurance defensible.

I am a qualified actuary and a data scientist. Thirteen years in life insurance — now in life reinsurance, building predictive underwriting that runs in production. In the open, I build the things that question is short of: data with a known answer, tests that count mistakes, and engines whose reasoning an auditor could actually read.

The starting point is one sentence: a decision an actuary cannot inspect is a decision an actuary cannot sign.

Why it matters

Three things have to be true before an AI output can enter an insurance decision

01 · MEASUREMENTGenerate the truth; never curate it

A curated answer key drifts and cannot be regenerated as the test grows. Both studies start from a deterministic generator, and are scored by a scorer that counts false alarms rather than rewarding the model for talking more.

02 · GOVERNANCEEvery claim ships the bytes behind it

Frozen harness snapshots, prompt hashes, decisions logged in JSONL, sealed manifests per scenario. A number without the instrument that produced it is an anecdote — mine included.

03 · ACCOUNTABILITYAgents propose; a named human decides

Escalation rules, permissions and conservatism belong in the architecture, not in the prompt wording. Stability is often bought with a risk-appetite decision, and somebody should sign that.

The journey

What open-sourcing all this has meant

I started publishing for a dull reason: I wanted the work to be checkable. A CV can say anything. A repository with a generator, a scorer and a log either supports the claim or it doesn't — and I would rather be argued with against evidence than admired against a summary.

It turned into something else. Everything here was built in broken time — forty minutes between a full-time job and childcare — which meant nothing could need me to sit and watch it. So I stopped writing code line by line and started designing loops I could delegate, then measuring what came out, because I could not inspect the models or my own code the way I used to. That is the same three-part discipline the profession needs: delegate the work, verify the behaviour, keep the sign-off. The constraint was the teacher.

It also made me a better actuary. Reading a benchmark result, a variance distribution or a vendor's model card is a different task once you have been the one who had to produce all three honestly.

The habit of shipping the bytes behind a number came from being wrong in public and having the record prove which part was wrong.

02Failures are the useful part

A run that retried forever, a tool nobody used, two claims that died at their noise floor. Every one changed a design decision.

03Architecture, not heroics

Delegation, resumable runs, handoff documents and a phone that beeps when the server frees up beat long nights and good intentions.

Who I am, plainly

I came up through the unglamorous end of the industry. I started at Zurich in 2013 as an actuarial analyst writing VBA, learned Python and SQL on the job rather than at university, and spent seven years integrating datasets that had been left inside other people's systems. Resolution Life was five more years of monthly performance papers to a CEO, a CFO and external auditors — a fast education in the difference between an impressive analysis and one that survives a question from someone whose job is to disbelieve it. I became a Fellow of the Actuaries Institute in 2021, and I am now a senior data scientist in life reinsurance at Pacific Life Re, where the work is productionised predictive underwriting: out-of-time validation, threshold optimisation against a stated risk tolerance, and approvals from underwriting, pricing, risk, model risk, legal and information security.

That route left me with an awkward combination for the rooms I usually sit in. I can build the pipeline, tune the model and argue about feature selection; I can also tell you which paragraph of a prudential standard the result has to satisfy, and what an auditor will ask for at 4pm on a Friday. When large language models got good enough to read an experience study, that combination turned into a question I could not leave alone: what would have to be true before one of these could be pointed at a decision I might have to sign?

The honest answer is that most of what is published about AI in insurance is a demonstration wearing the clothes of evidence. So I build the missing parts — data with a known answer, benchmarks that count mistakes, engines that log what they decided — and I publish the failures alongside the results, because the failures are where the governance actually lives.

What I am like to work with: I will ask for the denominator before the headline, I would rather scope a thing small and finish it than propose it well, and I am more useful in the morning than in a committee. The side projects are the tell — a Raspberry Pi running a particle-life society, a pixel-art generator that critiques its own output, two browser games with no dependencies — because they all obey the same rule the insurance work does: pre-register the question, publish the noise floor, keep the receipts.

What I am actually trying to do

Insurance is a profession that already audits itself: experience studies, mortality investigations, reserve adequacy, a signed opinion at the end of it. Machine learning enters that profession carrying a promise — pattern recognition at portfolio scale — and a problem: nobody can sign what they cannot inspect.

So the work here is aimed at one narrow question: what would have to be true for an actuarial AI system to be governable? The answer I keep arriving at is that three things are non-negotiable — a ground truth you generate rather than curate, a scorer that counts false alarms, and a byte-exact record of the instrument behind every claim. Everything on this site exists to demonstrate those three things, or to supply a component of them.

Five things I hold to, and where you can check them

01

Ground truth gets generated, not curated

A curated answer key drifts and cannot be regenerated when you extend the test. 47 scenarios, seed 42, pinned generator commit, sealed manifests.

generatorbench
02

Report the false alarms, and the cost

Accuracy can be bought by talking more. Every result here carries precision and per-answer tokens and wall time in the same row, not in an appendix.

findings ledger
03

Receipts over claims

Frozen harness snapshots, decision logs in JSONL, per-pass manifests, an experiment index that records the fixes deliberately not applied.

Pixel_Storybookunderwriter
04

Humans keep the accountability

Agents propose, analyse and explain; a named actuary or underwriter decides. Escalation, thresholds and permissions are structural, not prompt wording.

design principles
05

Open is a governance choice

The tools that decide which risks are acceptable should be readable by auditors, regulators and the profession. Closed-source underwriting engines are a black box.

Currently

  • Extending the benchmark's surface. Claims A/E was the first surface, not the limit. Lapse and persistency, expense A/E, and morbidity need new scenario families and a new section of stats_pack.py — not a new benchmark.
  • A fresh held-out exam. The published held-out split is spent, so the next number needs unseen scenarios with fresh controls.
  • A real jail for tool calls. The current sandbox is advisory: rlimits or a container with --network none, a read-only data mount, write-only output.
  • Turning the dashboard and the underwriter into scorable subjects using the bench's harness, so their claims sit at the same evidentiary standard.
